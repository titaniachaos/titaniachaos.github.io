# A hundred thousand billion arrangements

In 1961 Raymond Queneau cut ten sonnets into fourteen strips each and bound
them so that any line could follow any other: *Cent mille milliards de poèmes*,
10<sup>14</sup> poems, more than a reader could finish in a lifetime. What made
it work was not the cutting. Every line in a given position shared the rhyme
and the metre of every other, so no substitution could produce nonsense.

This archive was already cut into strips. Nineteen sections across four pages
each ask it a question in a closed vocabulary of fourteen words, and every
photograph that answers a question is, by construction, an answer to it. The
vocabulary is the rhyme scheme, and the arrangements number 1.33 × 10<sup>29</sup> — fifteen orders of magnitude past Queneau.

## What it will not do

Every line here was written by somebody who looked at the photograph. This
recombines those words and invents none of its own: nothing is generated, and
nothing is described by a machine that has not seen it. Fifty-one of these
frames have other people in them, and describing a picture you have not seen is
how you end up asserting who is in it.

An arrangement is not stored anywhere. The address is an index into the
archive, so the same number is the same arrangement on any machine, today and
in ten years, with nothing keeping a note of it. That is why it sits in the
URL, and why it is worth sharing.
